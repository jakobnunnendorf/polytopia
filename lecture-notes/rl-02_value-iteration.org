The goal of this lecture note is to compute, for every tile of a small
Polytopia map, how good it is for a rider to stand there when a village
is waiting to be captured.  The method is /value iteration/, from
Lecture 2 of the Reinforcement Learning course.  The lecture states it
in one line; here that line is turned into an OCaml function, tested,
run, and looked at.

* The map

The map has 8 columns and 6 rows.  Each character is one tile:

| character | terrain  |
|-----------+----------|
| ~.~       | field    |
| ~F~       | forest   |
| ~M~       | mountain |
| =~=       | water    |

* The rider

A rider has a movement of 2.  The rules of the game that matter here are
the following ones (=docs/shardlands/RULES.md=, Sections 2.1, 4.1
and 4.2):

- in one turn, a rider takes up to two steps, each to one of the 8
  neighbouring tiles;
- entering a forest ends the move;
- a mountain can only be entered with the Climbing technology, which our
  rider does not have; and
- water cannot be entered at all by a land unit without a port.

Everything else is left out: there are no roads, no other units, and so
no zone of control.

Before writing the function that lists where a rider can end its turn,
here is its unit-test function:

#+begin_src ocaml
let test_rider_moves candidate =
      (* in the open, two steps in every direction the map allows: *)
  let b0 = (candidate (0, 0) = [(0, 1); (0, 2); (1, 0); (1, 1); (1, 2); (2, 0); (2, 1); (2, 2)])
      (* in the thick of the forest, every first step ends the move: *)
  and b1 = (candidate (3, 0) = [(2, 0); (2, 1); (3, 1); (4, 0); (4, 1)])
      (* next to the village, the village is within reach: *)
  and b2 = List.mem village (candidate (5, 0))
      (* behind the mountain, it is not: *)
  and b3 = not (List.mem village (candidate (5, 2)))
  in b0 && b1 && b2 && b3;;
#+end_src

And here is the function, in three small steps: which tiles can be
entered, which tiles are one step away, and which tiles are one turn
away.

#+begin_src ocaml
let on_the_map (x, y) =
  0 <= x && x < width && 0 <= y && y < height;;

let enterable p =
  match terrain_at p with
  | Field | Forest | Village -> true
  | Mountain | Water -> false;;

let ends_the_move p =
  terrain_at p = Forest;;

let steps_from (x, y) =
  List.filter (fun q -> on_the_map q && enterable q)
    [(x - 1, y - 1); (x, y - 1); (x + 1, y - 1);
     (x - 1, y);                 (x + 1, y);
     (x - 1, y + 1); (x, y + 1); (x + 1, y + 1)];;

let rider_moves p =
  let first_steps = steps_from p
  in let second_steps = List.concat_map
                          (fun q -> if ends_the_move q then [] else steps_from q)
                          first_steps
     in List.sort_uniq compare
          (List.filter (fun q -> q <> p) (first_steps @ second_steps));;

let () = assert (test_rider_moves rider_moves);;
#+end_src

#+begin_src toplevel
rider_moves (0, 0);;
rider_moves (3, 0);;
#+end_src

In words: from the top-left corner, the rider can end its turn on 8
tiles; from the middle of the forest, on 5 tiles only, all of them
forest.

* The game as a Markov decision process

- A *state* $x$ is the tile the rider stands on.
- An *action* $a$ is the tile on which the rider ends its turn: one of
  the tiles listed by ~rider_moves~, or $x$ itself, since a rider may
  stay put.
- The *transition* is deterministic: after action $a$, the rider stands
  on $a$.  In the notation of the lecture, $P(y|x,a) = 1$ if $y = a$,
  and $0$ otherwise.
- The *reward* $r(x,a)$ is 1 if $a$ is the village, and 0 otherwise.
  Arriving at the village ends the ride, so the village itself is worth
  0: there is nothing more to gain from it.
- The *discount factor* $\gamma$ says how much a turn of waiting costs:
  a village reached one turn later is worth $\gamma$ times as much.

#+begin_src ocaml
let reward _x a =
  if a = village then 1. else 0.;;
#+end_src

The optimal value function $V^*$ says how much each tile is worth to a
rider who rides as well as possible.  It satisfies the Bellman
optimality equations [EMAI RL 2.0 p.56: Bellman optimality equations]:

\[
V^*(x) = \max_a \Big\{ r(x,a) + \gamma \sum_y P(y|x,a)\, V^*(y) \Big\}
\]

For our rider, the sum has exactly one non-zero term, the one where $y = a$:

\[
V^*(x) = \max_a \big\{ r(x,a) + \gamma\, V^*(a) \big\}
\]

In words: a tile is worth as much as the best tile within reach,
discounted by one turn, plus the reward for getting there.

* The Bellman optimality operator

The lecture reads the right-hand side of the equation as an /operator/,
$T^*$, that maps a function $f$ on the states to another function $T^*f$
on the states [EMAI RL 2.0 p.94: Bellman optimality operator]:

\[
(T^*f)(x) = \max_a \Big\{ r(x,a) + \gamma \sum_y P(y|x,a)\, f(y) \Big\}
\]

A function on the tiles is represented as an array of rows, like the
map.  The function that is 0 everywhere is our starting point:

#+begin_src ocaml
let value_at f (x, y) =
  f.(y).(x);;

let zero_value =
  Array.make_matrix height width 0.;;
#+end_src

And here is $T^*$, for a given $\gamma$.  The fold computes the maximum
over the actions, starting with the action of staying put:

#+begin_src ocaml
let bellman gamma f =
  let backup p a = reward p a +. gamma *. value_at f a
  in Array.init height (fun y ->
         Array.init width (fun x ->
             let p = (x, y)
             in if p = village || not (enterable p)
                then 0.
                else List.fold_left
                       (fun best a -> max best (backup p a))
                       (backup p p)
                       (rider_moves p)));;
#+end_src

To look at a function on the tiles, we print it as a table.  A forest
tile is marked with ~F~ after its value, and the tiles the rider cannot
stand on show their terrain instead of a value:

#+begin_src ocaml
let show f =

    (fun y row ->
      Array.iteri
        (fun x a ->
          match terrain_at (x, y) with
          | Mountain -> print_string "   M  "

          | Forest   -> Printf.printf " %.2fF" a
          | Field    -> Printf.printf " %.2f " a)
        row;

    f;;
#+end_src

* Unit tests for value iteration

To compare two functions on the tiles, the lecture uses their largest
difference over all states, $\| V - V' \|_\infty$:

#+begin_src ocaml
let distance v w =
  let differences = Array.map2
                      (Array.map2 (fun a b -> abs_float (a -. b)))
                      v w
  in Array.fold_left (Array.fold_left max) 0. differences;;
#+end_src

Value iteration takes a discount factor and a number of sweeps.  What
should it compute on our map?

#+begin_src ocaml
let close a b =
  abs_float (a -. b) < 1e-9;;

let test_value_iteration candidate =
  let v = candidate 0.9 20
  in    (* before the first sweep, nothing is worth anything: *)
  let b0 = (candidate 0.9 0 = zero_value)
        (* the ride ends at the village: *)
  and b1 = (value_at v village = 0.)
        (* one turn away: *)
  and b2 = close (value_at v (5, 0)) 1.
        (* four tiles away as the crow flies, but three turns away: *)
  and b3 = close (value_at v (3, 0)) (0.9 *. 0.9)
        (* the far corner, four turns away: *)
  and b4 = close (value_at v (0, 5)) (0.9 *. 0.9 *. 0.9)
        (* the result is a fixed point of the Bellman optimality operator: *)
  and b5 = close (distance (bellman 0.9 v) v) 0.
  in b0 && b1 && b2 && b3 && b4 && b5;;
#+end_src

The last test is the Bellman optimality equation itself, $V^* = T^*V^*$.

* Value iteration

Value iteration is the following algorithm
[EMAI RL 2.0 p.100: value iteration]: start from an arbitrary function
$V_0$, and compute

\[
V_{k+1} = T^* V_k
\]

for $k = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$  The lecture then states that $V_k$ converges
to $V^*$.

So $V_n$ is $T^*$ applied $n$ times to $V_0$.  Applying a function $n$
times is what a fold over a natural number does:

#+begin_src ocaml
let nat_fold_right zero_case succ_case n =
  let () = assert (n >= 0) in
  let rec visit i =
    if i = 0
    then zero_case
    else let ih = visit (pred i)
         in succ_case ih
  in visit n;;

let value_iteration gamma n =
  nat_fold_right zero_value (bellman gamma) n;;

let () = assert (test_value_iteration value_iteration);;
#+end_src

The definition of ~value_iteration~ is the line of the lecture, read
aloud: the zero case is $V_0$, and the successor case is $T^*$.

#+begin_src toplevel
show (value_iteration 0.9 20);;
#+end_src

#+begin_src scene :pic rl-02_the-values
picture (value_iteration 0.9 20)
#+end_src

* Riding greedily

Knowing $V^*$ is enough to ride well: an optimal policy picks, in each
state, an action that attains the maximum in the Bellman optimality
equation [EMAI RL 2.0 p.57: optimal policy].  For our rider:

\[
\pi^*(x) \in \arg\max_a \big\{ r(x,a) + \gamma\, V^*(a) \big\}
\]

#+begin_src ocaml
let greedy gamma v p =
  let backup a = reward p a +. gamma *. value_at v a
  in List.fold_left
       (fun best a -> if backup a > backup best then a else best)
       p
       (rider_moves p);;

let ride gamma v p =
  let rec visit p fuel =
    if p = village || fuel = 0
    then [p]
    else p :: visit (greedy gamma v p) (pred fuel)
  in visit p (width * height);;
#+end_src

The function ~ride~ lists the tiles on which the rider ends its turns,
from a given tile to the village:

#+begin_src toplevel
ride 0.9 (value_iteration 0.9 20) (0, 5);;
ride 0.9 (value_iteration 0.9 20) (0, 0);;
#+end_src

* The contraction, measured

Why does repeating $T^*$ converge?  The key idea of the lecture is that
$T^*$ is a /contraction/: for any two functions $V$ and $V'$
[EMAI RL 2.0 p.101: contraction],

\[
\| T^*V - T^*V' \|_\infty \le \gamma\, \| V - V' \|_\infty
\]

Let us measure it.  First on two successive sweeps, which are a
particular $V$ and $V'$:

#+begin_src ocaml
let successive_distances gamma n =
  List.init n
    (fun k -> distance (value_iteration gamma (succ k)) (value_iteration gamma k));;
#+end_src

#+begin_src toplevel
successive_distances 0.9 8;;
successive_distances 0.5 8;;
#+end_src

In words: each sweep moves the table by $\gamma$ times as much as the
sweep before, and after four sweeps it does not move at all.  (The
fourth number is 0.729, as closely as a floating-point number can say
it.)

Then on pairs of functions that have nothing to do with the game, with
values drawn at random between 0 and 1:

#+begin_src ocaml
let random_value seed =
  let state = Random.State.make [| seed |]
  in Array.init height (fun _ ->
         Array.init width (fun _ -> Random.State.float state 1.));;

let contraction_ratio gamma v w =
  distance (bellman gamma v) (bellman gamma w) /. distance v w;;
#+end_src

#+begin_src toplevel
contraction_ratio 0.9 (random_value 1) (random_value 2);;
contraction_ratio 0.9 (random_value 3) (random_value 4);;
contraction_ratio 0.9 (random_value 5) (random_value 6);;
#+end_src

In words: the ratio stays below $\gamma = 0.9$, as promised, and for
random pairs it is well below it.  The bound is a ceiling.  The
successive sweeps above are a case where the ceiling is touched.

* As the crow flies

A rider that only knows its distance to the village, counted in tiles,
would estimate the number of turns by dividing this distance by 2 and
rounding up.  The table knows better.  For which tiles is the crow too
optimistic?

#+begin_src ocaml
let turns_of_value gamma a =
  1 + int_of_float (Float.round (log a /. log gamma));;

let turns_as_the_crow_flies (x, y) =
  let (vx, vy) = village
  in (max (abs (vx - x)) (abs (vy - y)) + 1) / 2;;

let all_tiles =
  List.concat_map
    (fun y -> List.init width (fun x -> (x, y)))
    (List.init height (fun y -> y));;

let too_optimistic gamma v =
  List.filter
    (fun p -> enterable p
              && p <> village
              && turns_as_the_crow_flies p < turns_of_value gamma (value_at v p))
    all_tiles;;
#+end_src

#+begin_src toplevel
too_optimistic 0.9 (value_iteration 0.9 20);;
List.length (too_optimistic 0.9 (value_iteration 0.9 20));;
List.length (List.filter (fun p -> enterable p && p <> village) all_tiles);;
#+end_src

In words: on 12 of the 41 tiles where a rider can stand, the distance in
tiles promises one turn fewer than the ride takes.  Eleven of them are
in the four western columns, on the far side of the forest.  The
twelfth, ~(5, 2)~, is two tiles away from the village, behind the
mountain.

* Food for thought

- The lecture promises that $V_k$ gets closer to $V^*$ by a factor
  $\gamma$ at each sweep, forever.  Here, the table stops moving after
  four sweeps, and the distance is exactly 0.  Is the lecture wrong, or
  is our map special?  (Hint: [EMAI RL 2.0 p.64: Bellman and Dijkstra].)
- What is the smallest number of sweeps that passes the unit tests?
  What does this number say about the map?
- What happens to the table as $\gamma$ gets closer to 1?  What happens
  to the rides?  And what if $\gamma$ is exactly 1?
- The values in the table are $1$, $\gamma$, $\gamma^2$ and $\gamma^3$.
  State the general formula for the value of a tile from which the
  village is $n$ turns away, and say why it holds.
- In the unit tests, why is ~value_at v village~ compared with ~=~, and
  the other values with ~close~?
