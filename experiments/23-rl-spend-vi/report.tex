\section{Question and hypothesis}
\textbf{The idea (David, 27 Nov 2026, issue \#166).} Simplify both the state and the actions. State: (cities, income,
units, techs) instead of (turn, cities, income, units, levels, techs). Actions: only the ways to spend stars,
0 economy, 1 army, 2 research; expansion stays the bot's default. Background (experiments 21, 22): one turn's boost
moves the outcome by about 15 points against a spread of about 1{,}500, and with 3--4 visits per state and action the
$Q$ values differ mostly by chance. On the 300{,}000 random-action games of experiment 21, the four features give
57{,}029 states (144 visits per state, about 48 per action with three actions) instead of 506{,}245 (16 per state).
Dropping the turn merges situations several turns apart (4.5 turns between a state's first and last turn, on
average), so David's choice is to run both versions, with and without the turn, on the same data.

\textbf{Prediction} (before any run of this experiment):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{Few, well-visited states.} Without the turn about 60{,}000 states, with it about 280{,}000; at min-count
        5 the policy has an entry for more than 97\% of visits without the turn, more than 90\% with it.
  \item \emph{The policy researches more.} Both chosen policies play research on more than 50\% of learner turns
        (21: 29.5\%; research is the only one of the three actions that helps when played every turn).
  \item \emph{The smaller state is the better one.} Without the turn the policy scores higher on the validation
        seeds than with it (more visits outweigh the lost information).
  \item \emph{Final test.} The better version passes the \#171 rule against \texttt{greedy-full-probe-perf} on the
        evaluation seeds with a mean gain of 200--450 points (21: +104), is better than 21's policy seed by seed, and
        comes within 150 points of research every turn (21: 352 behind).
  \item \emph{Known risk without the turn.} The model then has loops (a state can lead back to itself with a
        positive reward), so the values are only bounded by the cap of 30 sweeps where a loop is certain; the
        sweeps settle (last change below 1 point) at min-count 5 or more.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Against the idea:} if neither version beats 21's policy on the evaluation seeds, simplifying the state and
the actions does not help value iteration here.

\section{Mechanism}
\textbf{Actions} (\texttt{spend3} in \texttt{rl\_value::strategic}): 0 economy (build), 1 army (train, attack),
2 research; each multiplies the base bot's move-type factors of its types by 2 for one learner turn (the same
boosts as these three actions in experiments 15--22). There is no ``bot unchanged'' action: a state without a policy
entry plays the base bot unchanged (David's choice). Army keeps train and attack (David's choice).

\textbf{State} (\texttt{--features}): (cities, income, units, techs), and (turn, cities, income, units, techs).
Dropped features are 0 in the policy table and in the lookup.

\textbf{Value iteration} as in experiment 21 ($Q(s,a) = $ mean of $r + V(s')$, $V(s) = \max_{a: n(s,a) \ge m}
Q(s,a)$, fallback to the mean over all transitions, $\gamma = 1$). With the turn, one backward sweep from the last
turn is exact. Without it, the same projected state occurs at different turns and can lead back to itself, so the
sweeps repeat (Jacobi, from $V = 0$) until the largest change is below $10^{-6}$, at most 30 times (the game's length
in learner turns); the tool reports the number of sweeps and the last change.

\section{Behaviour}
With the turn, the policy plays economy 21.2\%, army 36.3\% and research 35.9\% of learner turns on the evaluation
seeds; 6.6\% of turns are in a state without an entry and play the base bot. Without the turn it plays economy
37.9\%, army 41.3\%, research 16.2\% (4.6\% base bot): it mostly picks the two actions that leave the state unchanged
more often, and researches least, although research is the only action that helps when played every turn.

\section{Interpretation}
\textbf{Prediction 1 (about 60{,}000 and 280{,}000 states, coverage above 97\% and 90\% at $m=5$): right} (56{,}426
and 271{,}447; 98.9\% and 93.7\%).

\textbf{Prediction 2 (research on more than half the turns): wrong} for both (35.9\% with the turn, 16.2\% without).

\textbf{Prediction 3 (the smaller state is the better one): wrong, clearly.} Without the turn the policy is worse
than the bot; with it, it is the best policy of the track.

\textbf{Prediction 4 (passes against probe-perf with +200 to +450, better than 21, within 150 of research every
turn): mostly right.} With the turn it passes (+165, slightly below the range), is significantly better than 21's
policy (+61, $p = 2.4\cdot10^{-4}$) and 22's (+47, $p = 0.0013$), but stays 291 points behind research every turn.

\textbf{Prediction 5 (without the turn the sweeps settle at $m \ge 5$): wrong.} They never settle.

\textbf{Why dropping the turn fails.} Without the turn the problem is no longer finite: a state can lead back to
itself, and the learned model lets a loop collect its reward again on every sweep. An action that leaves (cities,
income, units, techs) unchanged keeps the learner in such a loop; research always leaves it (techs go up by one).
So the turn-free values overrate staying in place and underrate research, which is exactly how the policy plays. The
turn is not just one more feature: it is the time index of the 30-turn horizon, which makes every state passed
through at most once and the backward sweep exact.

\textbf{Why the spending actions with the turn help.} Against 21 the state lost only levels, and the actions lost
bot and expand (expand was neutral in every check). Three actions instead of five split the data three ways (about
10 visits per state and action instead of 3--4), and the max is over fewer noisy estimates: the optimism of
$V$(start) at $m=5$ falls from +1{,}793 (21) to +1{,}194. The gain over 21 (+61) is significant but modest, and the
gap to research every turn remains: the policy still spreads its choices (army on 36\% of turns, although army every
turn equals the bot).

\section{Threats to validity}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Two changes at once.} State and actions changed together; the gain over 21 cannot be split between
        dropping levels and dropping bot and expand.
  \item \emph{The turn-free values are not converged.} They are 30-sweep values; a different cap would give
        different numbers, though the failure (loops overrate unchanged states) does not depend on the cap.
  \item \emph{Gate code not on \texttt{dev} yet} (PR \#198); the policy files name their action set and features, so
        the runs repeat once it is merged.
\end{itemize}

\section{Verdict and next step}
\textbf{Adopted for David's track} (with the turn): \texttt{policy-turn-m5.tsv} passes \texttt{rl-gate} against
\texttt{greedy-full-probe-perf} (+165; information under the pass rule) and is significantly better than the
policies of experiments 21 and 22 seed by seed. It replaces them as David's RL v2 candidate, to be gated against RL
v1 (experiment 14). \textbf{Not kept}: the state without the turn.

Next step ideas (one per experiment): (a) actions held for several turns (larger effect per decision); (b) only two
actions, research and the bot; (c) data from research-heavy play.

\section{Notes for the paper}
Claim: the turn is what makes value iteration work on this state: dropping it turns the 30-turn problem into one
with loops, the learned values overrate actions that leave the state unchanged, and the policy loses to the bot
(-86). With the turn, three spending actions and five features give the best policy of the track (+165 against the
bot, 55.9\% of seeds better, significantly better than the five-action policy), still 291 points behind ``research
every turn''.
